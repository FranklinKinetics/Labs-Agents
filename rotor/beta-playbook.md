# Rotor Beta Playbook

Oct 16, 2026 · @Brandon

## Overview

The Rotor beta distills one company's strategy into the few choices, trade-offs and beliefs that drive decisions, in a form its people and its AI tools can act on, then tests it with one team for a full quarter. It runs about four and a half months: a five-to-six-week build, a 12-week live run, and a review. It is sold through Franklin Kinetics as a fixed-fee founding beta.

The beta runs AI-native and mostly asynchronous. Only three sessions are live: the kickoff, the Mirror and the review. Interviews, document intake, synthesis, testing and progress updates happen in between, on the client's time and yours. See How the beta runs under Beta structure.

What the client gets:

- A **Rotor Sheet**: one schwerpunkt with the evidence that shows it's working, five to seven guardrails with worked examples, eight to twelve living models with tripwires, a margin target, and a **language map** that expresses all of it in the company's own metaphors.
- **One strategy, rendered three ways**: text for the AI tools, a visual people can take in at a glance, and a two-to-three-minute strategy video narrated by the CEO.
- That sheet, together with the Rotor Core (the framework's reasoning models written for an AI), **loaded into the AI tools they already use**, with guidance that stays quiet unless it matters and always assumes the person knows more about their situation than the AI does.
- A full-quarter **beta with one team**, measured against a comparison team, and a review showing where strategy held, where it caused friction, and which models are already under strain.

Everything the client sees comes from their Rotor Sheet. Your library builds it and is never handed over; the Rotor Core travels only into the AI tools, under licence.

Who it is for: private firms of roughly 20 to 200 people whose leaders already use AI tools at work and feel that strategy does not survive contact with daily work.

## Selling the beta

Sell the problem leaders already feel, show the live demo, and close on a small fixed-fee beta with a clear exit.

### Who to approach first

- Owner-led or founder-led firms, 20 to 200 people, where the CEO can say yes without a procurement process.
- Firms in fast-changing markets, where aligned speed matters most: technology, professional services, growth-stage companies. In slow, scale-driven industries, sell Rotor for coordination rather than speed.
- Firms already running a planning system such as EOS, Scaling Up or OKRs. They have proven they value strategic discipline and already hold most of a Rotor Sheet's raw material.
- Leaders who already use ChatGPT, Claude or Copilot themselves.
- Firms that recently ran a strategy offsite, raised money, hired a new executive, or are growing fast. Those are the moments when strategy drifts.
- One beachhead first, such as Saskatchewan professional-services or technology firms, so references and word of mouth build on each other.

### The problem, in their words

- "We agreed on priorities in the spring. By summer everyone was busy with other things."
- "Our strategy lives in a deck nobody opens."
- "Our people are using AI every day, and it has no idea what we're trying to do."

If a leader nods at any of these, you have a conversation.

### The pitches (60 seconds each)

Two pitches, one offer. Pick the one that matches what the leader is already worried about.

**Pitch A: strategy that doesn't stick.** Use it with leaders frustrated that plans fade after the offsite.

> Most companies put real effort into strategy, and it ends up in a document that doesn't shape daily decisions. Research in Harvard Business Review found only about a third of managers could name their company's top three priorities. Your people now make many of those decisions with AI tools that know nothing about your strategy. I help leadership teams distill their strategy to what actually drives decisions: the main effort, the trade-offs, and the beliefs it rests on, in your own company's language. Then I load it into the AI tools your people already use, so it quietly informs their work and flags when reality starts proving it wrong. The method builds on Porter, Rumelt and Bungay's work on mission command in business. The founding beta is a short build followed by a full quarter with one team, at a fixed fee.

**Pitch B: the room you're not in.** Use it with founders and CEOs who feel the company is getting too big for them to be in every decision.

> Every day, your people make hundreds of decisions you'll never see: which client gets priority, which discount gets approved, which project gets the extra hours. Those decisions are your real strategy. Most leaders assume their people decide the way they would. The research says otherwise. In one study, an insurance company's executives expected their experts to differ by about 10% when pricing the same case. The real gap was around 55%. That isn't bad people. It's people without a shared reference point. I give them one: the main effort, the trade-offs and the warning signs, distilled into a few clear choices, in your company's own language, available wherever decisions now get made, including the AI tools they think with. It doesn't decide for them; they know their situation better than anyone. It makes sure they decide with your intent in mind. Over one quarter, with one team, you'll see how your people decide when you're not in the room.

How the two differ:

|  | Pitch A | Pitch B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opens with | The strategy-execution gap | The leader's own reach: decisions made without them |
| Evidence | Sull, Homkes and Sull: about a third of managers can name their top three priorities | Kahneman, Sibony and Sunstein, *Noise*: an expected 10% gap between experts was really about 55% |
| Core image | A strategy deck nobody opens | The room you're not in |
| Best buyer | A CEO who runs offsites and planning cycles | A founder whose company has outgrown their personal involvement |
| Risk to manage | Sounds like ordinary strategy consulting | Can sound like control; stress that people keep the decision |

Either way, the offer is the same beta: a short build, then a full quarter. A good follow-up question for Pitch B: "Which decision made last month, without you, would you most like to have been in the room for?" Their answer usually names the first guardrail.

### Why you

Lead with the background that explains why you're the one to solve this:

> I'm a military flight instructor with an MBA. My job is teaching people to hold a complex situation in their heads and make good decisions under pressure. I also train fellow pilots in human performance, using mishap case studies to sharpen how they make sense of situations. Rotor brings that to business strategy, for your people and the AI tools they now work with.

Use the human performance line only after completing the December course. Keep all examples to general experience; no unit specifics.

### Positioning

- **Complement existing planning systems, don't compete with them.** "You have a written strategy. Your AI has never been briefed on it." Rotor makes an EOS or OKR plan testable and carried by AI.
- **Answer the do-it-yourself option directly.** "Uploading your strategy deck gives your AI a document. Rotor gives it judgment: the trade-offs, the boundaries, and the beliefs to watch."
- **Lead with the client's job, not the technology.** They want strategy that actually guides what people do. AI is how Rotor delivers it.
- **Business language first.** Say "main effort" and "directed opportunism"; introduce "schwerpunkt" as a signature term and always translate it.
- Sell the async design as respect for their time. "Your leaders contribute on their own schedule, in their own words. We meet three times, and every meeting decides something."

### Discovery questions

Ask these in the first meeting. Listen more than you talk.

1. If I asked five of your leaders separately what the company's main effort is this year, would I get the same answer?
2. When two good priorities collide, who decides, and how do people know which way to lean?
3. What did you decide at your last planning session that didn't happen?
4. What planning system do you use today: EOS, Scaling Up, OKRs, or something of your own?
5. Which AI tools are your people using today, officially or not?
6. What belief is your current strategy built on that would hurt most if it turned out wrong?
7. When this company is at its best, what is it like? (Listen for the metaphors.)
8. What would have to be true at the end of the first quarter for this to have been worth it?

### The demo

Open Claude or ChatGPT with the demo company's Rotor Sheet loaded. Ask three questions live:

1. A request that fits the strategy. The answer is simply good, with no mention of strategy.
2. A tempting project that pulls effort from the main effort. The AI adds one light line asking whether that's intended.
3. A plan that breaks a guardrail. The AI names the conflict and leaves the decision to the human.

Then say: "Imagine that, with your strategy, in every conversation your people have with AI."

## Organizational AI integration

Start with the lightest integration that works. Most betas need only the first level, and the client keeps control of their data at every level.

### Three levels of integration

| Level | What it is | Who runs it | Use when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Instructions | The Rotor Core plus the client's Rotor Sheet and language map, loaded into a Claude Project, custom GPT or Copilot agent | The client, set up by you in an afternoon | Every beta. Default. |
| 2. Shared connector | An MCP server holding the Rotor Sheet, connected to the company's AI tools | The client's IT team (self-hosted) | Several teams or several AI tools need the same sheet kept in sync |
| 3. Signal loop | The MCP server also logs anonymous tripwire hits, friction and repeated overrides for management reports | The client's IT team, possibly with a developer partner | Only after a successful beta, with the safeguards under Governance |

Levels 1 and 2 run the right side of the loop. Level 3 closes it, so the models learn from daily work.

Do not host client strategy data yourself. If a client wants level 2 or 3 and has no IT team, bring in a developer partner under a separate agreement.

### The Rotor Core

Every client's AI receives two files. The **Rotor Core** is the same for every client and teaches the AI how to reason with Rotor. The client's **Rotor Sheet** tells it what this organization has decided. Keeping them separate means the method improves for every client at once, while each client's content stays their own.

[embedded content: spinning-top schema · from the white paper]

This is the picture the Core gives the AI. A strong schwerpunkt and fast decision loops keep the top stable; friction slows it toward toppling.

- **What's in it:** an order of precedence (law and policy first, then the person's informed decision, then the Rotor Sheet, then the Core's defaults). Section A gives the AI the white paper's spinning-top schema as its internal picture of the organization: schwerpunkt as spindle, mass, the velocity of information, energy, friction, wobbles, the toppling threshold, shedding mass, internal disintegration, schwerpunkt strength, fractal tops, resonance and stewards. Section B turns that into fifteen operating models: schwerpunkt, guardrails, orientation and signal over noise, loop speed, living models, load-bearing models, aligned velocity, friction, margin, shedding mass, stewardship, passive publication, local knowledge, graded assertiveness and the signal loop. It ends with language and transparency rules, a never-do list and worked examples.
- **Written for a context window, not a reader:** imperative instructions, a short definition per model, then how to use it and what not to do, plus worked examples. It stays around 3,000 words so it can be loaded into every conversation.
- **Source:** the white paper (*Rotor Framework: A Visceral Schema of Human Organizations*, v1.1, November 2024), rewritten for machines and extended with the beta method. The white paper persuades people; the Core instructs an AI. Keep them consistent, and issue a new Core version whenever the white paper changes.
- **Versioning:** one Core for all clients, updated centrally. Record which version each client is running.
- **Ownership:** the white paper is published under a Creative Commons BY-NC-SA 4.0 licence, so its ideas are already open for non-commercial use. The Core, the beta method and your client library are what you license commercially. Clients' leadership and compliance staff may read the Core under confidentiality, and the licence limits it to internal use. See Protecting the Core.
- **Testing:** the red-team session tests the Core and the Rotor Sheet together.

Current draft: Rotor Core v0.2, reconciled with white paper v1.1.

### Protecting the Core

Anything loaded into a client's AI can be extracted: a determined user can get an AI to repeat its instructions, and IT staff can read the files directly. So protection comes from deciding what goes into the client's AI at all, then guarding the rest by contract, tracing and continued updates.

**Split every delivery into two layers.**

| Goes into the client's AI | Stays with you |
| --- | --- |
| The Rotor Core's operating principles: precedence, the schema in short form, graded assertiveness, local knowledge, transparency, the never-do list | The worked-example library and red-team dilemma bank |
| The client's own Rotor Sheet and language map | Industry templates and starting models |
| A few generic worked examples | Metaphor maps from past clients |
|  | Tuned phrasing and instruction variants |
|  | The interview, facilitation and diagnostic process |

The client gets the output of your proprietary work, a tuned Rotor Sheet, without the machinery that produced it. The principles are largely derived from the openly licensed white paper and published research, so exposing them costs little.

**Protect what you hand over.**

- **Licence terms:** internal use only; no sharing, resale or competing products built from it; confidentiality; return or deletion at the end of the engagement.
- **Copyright notice** on every version of the Core.
- **A fingerprint per client:** small, harmless wording differences in each client's copy, so a leaked copy can be traced to its source. Record each client's variant.

**Make a stale copy worth less than a live one.** License the Core as a subscription that includes new versions, new worked examples and tuning from quarterly reviews. A copied version freezes on the day it was taken.

**At the platform stage, keep the reasoning on your side.** The client's AI calls a Rotor-hosted service; your server runs the full Core and library privately and returns only a short judgment. The client never receives the Core text. This requires hosting client strategy data, so it waits for a technical partner, security review and insurance.

**Transparency without disclosure.** The publicity test is about how the AI behaves, not about your methods. All employees see the Rotor Sheet and a plain-language summary of how the AI behaves. Leadership and compliance staff can read the full Core under confidentiality.

Your real moat is the library, the skill in the room and the brand. Protect those carefully; don't spend effort hiding the rest.

### What the organization exposes

Expose intent widely, data narrowly, and actions very narrowly.

- **Intent** (schwerpunkt, guardrails, models): every employee's AI. This is the Rotor Sheet.
- **Reference knowledge** (policies, product facts): broad, with confidential items held back. Outside beta scope.
- **Operational data** (customers, finances, people): by role only. Outside beta scope.
- **Actions** (send, approve, spend): the AI drafts, a human approves. Outside beta scope.

### How the AI behaves

One rule: stay quiet unless it matters, and speak up in proportion to the stakes.

1. **Ambient** (most of the time): strategy shapes which options come first and how trade-offs are framed. It is not mentioned, but the AI explains openly if asked why.
2. **Nudge** (when relevant): one light question, such as "This would pull time from the main effort. Is there something here that makes it worth it?"
3. **Explicit** (real conflict): the AI names the clash plainly, then hands the decision back: "This crosses the guardrail on discounting. You may know something that justifies it. Your call."

[embedded content: graded assertiveness · three levels]

Each step up needs higher stakes. Whatever the level, the decision returns to the person.

**The person knows more than the AI.** Every nudge assumes the person knows more about their situation than the AI does. The AI holds the strategy; the person holds the context. This follows Hayek's point that the most important knowledge is local, and it guards against automation bias, where people over-trust confident machines and stop thinking. In practice:

- Nudges are questions, not corrections.
- Before nudging on something big, the AI asks what's driving the decision. The answer often resolves the apparent conflict.
- When someone knowingly overrides a nudge, the AI accepts it without arguing.
- Repeated overrides on the same guardrail are information, not non-compliance: the people closest to the work may be seeing that a model or guardrail is wrong.

This mirrors graded assertiveness in aviation crew resource management (often taught as PACE: probe, alert, challenge, emergency). The Rotor AI behaves like a well-briefed first officer, not a compliance officer.

Instruction text for the AI:

> Assume the person knows more about their situation than you do. You hold the strategy; they hold the context. Raise strategic considerations as questions, escalate only as far as the stakes require, and accept their decision. Use the company's own language from the language map. Treat repeated overrides as a sign the strategy may need updating, not as non-compliance.

The AI never hides that strategy is informing its answers. Apply the **publicity test**: publish the client's Rotor Sheet and a plain-language summary of how the AI behaves, so any employee can see how it is guided. Leadership and compliance staff can read the full Rotor Core under confidentiality. If leadership wouldn't publish an instruction, it doesn't go in.

### Governance

Agree these points in writing before the beta starts:

- **Author:** the leadership team owns the Rotor Sheet. One named executive approves every change.
- **Scope:** one Rotor Sheet per business unit and planning horizon. Corporate sets shared guardrails.
- **Version history:** every change is dated and kept, including retired models and the reason they were dropped. The visual and video are updated whenever the sheet changes.
- **Exclusions:** what never goes in, such as acquisitions, pending personnel decisions, legal matters, and unannounced financials.
- **Transparency:** employees receive a one-page note on what the AI carries and logs, and can read the Rotor Sheet and a plain-language summary of how the AI behaves.
- **Review cadence:** models reviewed at least quarterly; the schwerpunkt reviewed when a load-bearing model breaks.
- Interview data: full interview transcripts are read only by you, stored in the client's own AI platform, and deleted from your notes at the end of the engagement. What goes into the divergence summary is quoted without names unless the leader agrees.
- **Signal safeguards (level 3 only):** aggregate reports only, no report on any group smaller than five people, never used in performance reviews, disclosed in the employer's electronic monitoring policy where one is required (Ontario requires one at 25 or more employees), and reviewed for privacy before it starts.

When compliance staff ask, map these points to the Govern function of the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and to the ISO/IEC 42001 AI management system. Present Rotor as filling a gap inside existing AI governance, not as a new field.

## Beta structure

The beta runs about four and a half months: a five-to-six-week build, a full quarter of live use, and a review. Strategy needs a quarter of real use to show in behaviour, so the live run is the longest phase but the lightest for you. Two gates keep it from going live on a weak sheet or an untested AI.

[embedded content: Rotor beta · 3 live sessions, 2 gates]

The team goes live only after leadership signs off the sheet at the Mirror and the red-team check passes. The CEO briefs the beta team at go-live (a recorded video works), and a weekly flight log plus monthly AI pulses keep the change from fading. Async delivery should bring your time to roughly 20 to 25 hours in the build and 8 to 10 across the live run and review, including the video script. These are estimates; track real hours on the first beta and replace them.

### How the beta runs: three live sessions, everything else async

Only three moments are live. Everything else moves asynchronously, with AI doing the collecting, so leaders contribute on their own time and you can deliver around an unpredictable schedule.

Clients judge an engagement by three things: being heard, seeing their own thinking reflected back sharper, and visible momentum. None of them needs a shared calendar slot. So meetings are replaced with async conversation (an AI interviewer, voice memos, recorded replies), never with forms.

| Phase | Mode | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kickoff (60–90 min) | Live | Scope, measures and governance agreed; AI interviewer set up on the client's platform; the Mirror and the review booked now |
| Build, wave 1 (weeks 1–2) | Async | AI reads the existing strategy documents; AI interviews each leader on context and friction |
| Build, wave 2 (weeks 2–4) | Async | Follow-up interviews built from each leader's own wave 1 answers; divergence summary drafted |
| Build, wave 3 (weeks 4–5) | Async | Leaders react to draft schwerpunkt candidates and guardrails in short memos |
| The Mirror (2–3 hours) | Live | The distilled strategy reflected back in the leaders' own words; contested calls settled; Rotor Sheet signed off |
| Red-team (weeks 5–6) | Async | Beta team lead submits dilemmas; you run and annotate them; the lead rates the answers |
| Live run (12 weeks) | Async | Weekly flight log to the CEO, monthly AI pulses, AI-administered noise audit; optional 20-minute check-in |
| Beta review (90 min) | Live | Results against the measures; scale, adjust or stop |

Rules for the async core:

- **Sell responsiveness, not availability.** Promise a reply standard, such as every memo answered within two working days, instead of open meeting access.
- **Book all three live sessions at kickoff,** inside windows you set. Never let two clients' Mirrors land in the same week.
- **AI collects; you judge.** The AI runs interviews, intake, pulses and first-pass synthesis. You review every transcript and make the calls that clients pay for: the schwerpunkt, the Mirror, the sheet.
- **Platform-neutral by design.** The interviewer is a plain-text instruction pack with knowledge files that installs in a Claude Project, custom GPT, Gemini Gem or Copilot agent. Every session ends with a summary block in a fixed format, so synthesis works the same whichever AI ran it. The client chooses the platform.
- **Disclose before the first interview.** Tell leaders what is attributed and what is anonymized, that only you read full transcripts, and which vendor's AI holds them.
- **Scale in waves.** Start two or three clients together so prompt packs, review batches and the weekly rhythm are shared. Set the number per wave from your tracked hours, and protect synthesis time first.

The rotor-async-beta skill runs each step: engagement plan, prompt packs, interviewer brief, synthesis, flight logs, session prep and live-run pulses.

The interviewer's instructions, wave files, disclosure note and noise audit variant live in Rotor Interviewer Pack.

### Deliverables

- Divergence summary and harvested company language (after the wave 2 interviews)
- Resource test findings: where people, money and attention actually go versus the schwerpunkt
- Signed-off Rotor Sheet, including the language map
- One-page Rotor visual
- Strategy video (two to three minutes, CEO-narrated) and short follow-up clips per guardrail
- AI tools configured with the Rotor Core and the client's Rotor Sheet, tuned in the red-team session, with a plain-language behaviour summary published internally
- Employee transparency note
- Weekly flight logs to the CEO through the build and live run (a short written brief, with an optional phone video)
- Beta review report with recommended changes

### Success measures

The noise audit is the headline measure. Add two or three others at kickoff and measure them at the points shown.

| Measure | How to check it | When |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Noise audit | Give beta and comparison teams the same five realistic decision cases, presented by an AI interviewer that also asks for their reasoning, and measure how far apart their answers are | Before go-live and at the end of the quarter |
| Leader agreement | The AI interviewer asks each leader for the main effort in one sentence; count how many match | Wave 1 interviews and after the Mirror |
| Recall | A short AI pulse asks both teams to name the main effort and two guardrails | Weeks 2 and 12 of the live run |
| Where time goes | A one-week sample of the team's hours: the share spent on the main effort | Start and end of the live run |
| Decision speed | Time taken on two or three recurring decisions, such as quotes or approvals | Start and end of the live run |
| Guidance quality | Share of answers rated right rather than too pushy, too quiet or wrong | Red team, then monthly |
| Early warning | Tripwire signals and repeated overrides surfaced | Throughout |
| Friction found | Specific sources of delay named, each with an owner | Throughout |
| Decision log | Three to five moments where the guidance changed a call | Throughout |

Record baselines for both teams before kickoff. A comparison team that doesn't get Rotor separates the beta's effect from people simply improving because they're being watched. Report null results honestly; they protect your credibility. These are leading indicators: they move within a quarter and predict the lagging results leaders care about. Promise evidence of alignment and decision quality, not financial returns within the beta, and state expected financial effects as hypotheses for the quarterly reviews to test.

## Running the beta

Three live sessions carry the beta; the other steps run asynchronously between them. Each step below has its purpose, its agenda, and the words you can use.

### Live session 1: Kickoff (60–90 minutes, CEO and beta sponsor)

Purpose: agree scope, success measures, governance and logistics.

1. Confirm the beta team, a comparison team, the AI tools in use, and who owns the Rotor Sheet.
2. Agree two or three success measures (see Beta structure) and take baselines for both teams, including the noise audit cases.
3. Agree the exclusions list, the employee transparency note, and that the AI's instructions will be published internally.
4. Collect existing strategy material: plans, board decks, offsite notes, OKRs or EOS documents.
5. Collect the company's language: all-hands decks, founding stories, values statements, sayings people repeat.
6. Agree the interview disclosure: what is attributed, what is anonymized, that only you read full transcripts, and which AI platform holds them.
7. Install the AI interviewer on the client's chosen platform, agree your reply standard, and book the Mirror and the beta review now.

Opening line: "By the end of the first quarter we want a distilled strategy your leaders agree on, in use by one team, and evidence of whether it changed how they work."

### Async step: Leader interviews (AI-led, three waves, on each leader's own time)

Purpose: hear each leader's strategy separately, before anyone hears anyone else's. The differences are your first finding.

How it runs: each leader talks with the AI interviewer in two or three sessions of about 20 minutes, whenever suits them, by voice or text. Wave 1 covers the questions below. Wave 2 follow-ups are generated from each leader's own wave 1 answers, so the interview remembers them. The interviewer asks follow-ups on vague answers, assumes the leader knows more than it does, never advises, and closes each session by summarizing back what it heard. You review every transcript before anything enters the synthesis. Leaders who prefer can record voice memos to the same prompts.

Question bank (wave 1):

1. In one sentence, where is this company putting its main effort this year, and why?
2. What are we deliberately giving up or saying no to, to make that happen?
3. When two good things collide, such as a big new client versus serving existing ones, which way should people lean?
4. What do we believe about our market, customers or people that, if wrong, would change the plan?
5. What would tell you early that one of those beliefs is failing?
6. What slows this company down most? Where does work wait?
7. What did we decide in the past year that didn't stick? Why not?
8. When this company is at its best, what is it like? What image or story captures it?
9. Where did our money and people actually go last year? Does that match the main effort you just described?

After wave 2, write a two-page **divergence summary**: where leaders agree, where they differ, and the questions the Mirror must settle. Quote nobody by name. Add a short **language harvest**: the recurring images, phrases and stories, and the two or three candidate metaphors that could carry the whole structure.

Wave 3: send each leader the draft schwerpunkt candidates and guardrails with two or three reaction prompts, such as "What in this feels wrong, or missing?" Their short replies show what is already agreed and what the Mirror must settle.

Riffing tip: your recorded replies to leaders are the place to riff. Fast metaphors help leaders find words for what they mean. Save the commitment to one metaphor for the Mirror.

### Live session 2: The Mirror (2–3 hours, leadership team)

Purpose: reflect the strategy back in the leaders' own words, settle what wave 3 left contested, and sign off the Rotor Sheet. Most lines arrive already converged, so the session spends its time on the hard calls. This is the highest-value moment of the beta; protect its prep time.

| Time | Block | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00 | The reveal | The problem in their words; the divergence summary; the resource test findings, prepared async. Ground rule: the goal is a strategy short and clear enough for a new hire or an AI to act on. |
| 0:25 | Schwerpunkt | Present the candidates wave 3 narrowed. Debate and converge on one. Add two or three indicators of evidence it's working. |
| 0:55 | Guardrails | Ratify the uncontested ones and settle the contested ones. Run each through the five tests; confirm a worked example for each. |
| 1:35 | Break |  |
| 1:45 | Living models and margin | Confirm the beliefs, tripwires and owners drafted async. Mark the load-bearing ones. Agree the planning buffer and the top three sources of friction. |
| 2:15 | Language and close | Choose one dominant metaphor from the harvest. Read the full Rotor Sheet aloud, in the company's language. Confirm the owner and the date of the first review. |

Facilitation lines that help:

- "If this sentence were removed, would anyone decide anything differently?"
- "What good thing does this guardrail make us give up?"
- "How would we know in three months that this belief is wrong?"
- "Would a new hire on day one know what to do with this?"

### The five tests

Run every line of the Rotor Sheet through these. A line that fails is rewritten or cut. Brevity is the result of distillation, not a format rule: keep what changes a decision. If the sheet still runs long, the strategy usually isn't clear yet.

| Test | Question | Fails | Passes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Decision | Would this change a choice someone makes? | "We value innovation." | "New products must reach a paying customer within 90 days." |
| Trade-off | Does it name something good we give up? | "Favour quality." | "Favour existing clients over new logos." |
| New hire | Could someone apply it on day one without asking? | "Be strategic about partnerships." | "We partner only where we keep the client relationship." |
| Hindsight | Would it have pointed the right way on five real past decisions? | Points the wrong way on the deal you regret | Points the right way |
| Tripwire | Does each model have a signal that would prove it wrong? | "Customers love our service." | "Clients renew on trust; tripwire: two lost renewals on price in a quarter." |

### One model, many bridges

The Rotor model is fixed. It describes how organizations work, the way the laws of flight describe how an aircraft works. What changes is the bridge to each person. A flight instructor teaching a loop, low-level navigation or radio calls keeps the physics constant, but reaches for analogies the student already understands: a sport they played, an instrument, cooking. Rotor works the same way.

[embedded content: one model, many bridges]

- **With the organization:** adopt its dominant metaphor and attach its own sayings to it. This is the language map below.
- **With individuals:** in interview prompts, your recorded replies, the Mirror and the video, tailor examples to what each leader already knows. A former athlete may grasp the main effort as the game plan; an engineer may grasp margin as a safety factor.
- **Never bend the model to fit the analogy.** When an analogy breaks down, drop it and choose another. The principles stay put.

Listening for these bridges is part of the interview. Ask about backgrounds and interests, not only the business.

### Choosing the metaphor

A client's metaphor is the skin; Rotor's structure is the skeleton. Harvest candidates in the interviews, then choose one in the Mirror.

- **Test it:** a good metaphor maps onto all six elements. If it covers only one or two, it's decoration.
- **Pick one:** stitch the company's smaller sayings onto a single dominant image. Several competing metaphors confuse people.
- **Check its hidden logic:** war metaphors imply enemies and casualties; family metaphors make hard decisions feel like betrayal. Choose deliberately.

| Rotor element | Sports team | Expedition | Restaurant kitchen |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Schwerpunkt | The season's game plan | The summit | Tonight's signature dish |
| Guardrails | Plays we always and never run | Turnaround rules | House standards |
| Living models | Scouting reports | Weather forecast | What we think diners want |
| Tripwires | Opponent changes formation | Pressure drops | Plates coming back |
| Friction | Turnovers and penalties | Bad terrain | Bottlenecks at the grill |
| Margin | Bench depth | Spare oxygen and rest days | Prep done before service |

The language map then feeds the AI (so a nudge sounds like "that's off the game plan"), the visual and the video.

### The strategy video and visual

One strategy is rendered three ways from the same Rotor Sheet: text for the AI, a visual for people to take in at a glance, and a short video for learning it. The visual extends people's limited working memory; the video makes the strategy memorable.

- **Length:** two to three minutes, plus a 20-to-30-second clip per guardrail for team meetings and onboarding.
- **Voice:** the CEO narrates. Setting orientation is the CEO's job, and hearing it in their voice signals that it matters. You write the script.
- Recording: the CEO records the voiceover on their own time from your script. No session needed.
- **Structure:** the visual builds piece by piece: schwerpunkt first, then each guardrail with a quick example, then the living models and tripwires, ending on the full picture.
- **World:** the client's chosen metaphor sets the visual world, such as a mountain, a field or a kitchen.
- **Content:** choices, not slogans. "When X competes with Y, we lean toward X. Here's an example."
- **Repetition:** one viewing is forgotten within days. Show it at onboarding, at quarterly reviews and whenever the sheet changes.
- **Versioning:** update the video when the sheet changes. An outdated video is worse than none.
- **Production:** use one animated template that reads the Rotor Sheet and generates the visuals, plus the CEO's voiceover. Keep it internal to the client.

### Async step: Red-team (you and the beta team lead)

Purpose: test the loaded AI before the team relies on it.

How it runs: the team lead sends the dilemmas in writing. You run them, annotate the answers and send them back for the lead to rate. A 30-minute call is optional, only where the lead's ratings disagree with yours.

1. The team lead writes ten realistic dilemmas the team actually faces, including some where the person has local knowledge that justifies breaking a guardrail.
2. Put each to the AI and record the level it responds at: ambient, nudge or explicit.
3. The lead marks each answer as right, too pushy, too quiet, or wrong. Check that nudges are phrased as questions, that the AI asks about context before pushing, and that it accepts an informed override.
4. Check that the AI speaks in the company's language from the language map.
5. Tune the wording of the guardrails and instructions, then re-run the failures.

Target before go-live: most answers ambient, nudges only where a reasonable leader would nudge, informed overrides accepted without argument, and no wrong calls on the explicit cases. The session works like a premortem: imagine the AI has annoyed or misled the team, and find out why before it happens.

### Between sessions: flight logs and pulses

These replace the old week 4 and week 8 check-in meetings.

- **Weekly flight log.** Same day every week, to the CEO, under 300 words: what we heard, what's taking shape, what's next, one question. Add a 3-to-5-minute phone video when there is something to show.
- **Monthly AI pulse.** Three questions to the beta team through their AI tool: what they found themselves deciding differently, where the guidance helped or got in the way, and the main effort in their own words.
- **Noise audit by AI.** The decision cases are presented conversationally, before go-live and at the end of the quarter, so you collect the reasoning behind each answer as well as the answer.
- **Memo replies.** Every memo or question gets a reply within your stated standard.
- **Optional check-in.** A 20-minute call only when the CEO asks for one or a pulse shows trouble.

### Live session 3: Beta review (90 minutes, leadership team)

Purpose: show what happened and decide what comes next.

1. Results against the agreed success measures, beta team versus comparison team.
2. Examples where the AI's guidance helped, annoyed, or missed.
3. Model health: which tripwires fired, and what repeated overrides suggest about the guardrails.
4. Resource test follow-up: has spending or staffing moved toward the main effort?
5. Friction found during the beta.
6. Changes to the Rotor Sheet, visual and video.
7. Decision: stop, extend to more teams, or move to the quarterly review retainer.

### Optional module: the strategy debrief (half day, leadership team)

A sensemaking workshop built on how aviation and safety fields study failure. Leaders analyze public cases, then apply the same lens to their own strategy: what did they believe, which tripwires fired, and why didn't anyone act?

| Case | What happened | Lesson for Rotor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mann Gulch fire (1949) | Foreman Wagner Dodge lit an escape fire and lay down in it; his crew didn't understand his intent and ran. Thirteen died. | Intent must be shared before the crisis, not explained during it. |
| Tenerife collision (1977) | The KLM captain began takeoff without clearance; ambiguous radio language and a steep authority gradient kept the crew from stopping him. | Psychological safety and plain language. |
| Air France 447 (2009) | Iced airspeed sensors disconnected the autopilot, and the crew lost situational awareness. | A warning for organizations handing more thinking to automation and AI. |
| A corporate failure (e.g. Nokia, Kodak, Blockbuster) | Leaders held beliefs that stopped being true. | Living models, tripwires and load-bearing beliefs. |

Frame it with Weick's sensemaking work on Mann Gulch and Tenerife, Reason's Swiss cheese model of how failures line up, and Westrum's typology of how information flows in organizations. Use only public cases and published frameworks. Nothing from DND investigations or course materials.

## Learning loop and the alignment diagnostic

The first betas teach you which parts of the work need you; the diagnostic gives you volume. Automate a step only after the evidence says it's safe, and never automate the schwerpunkt call.

### What a few betas can and can't tell you

| Question | Can a few betas answer it? | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Does Rotor improve decisions? | No | Three results can't separate Rotor from the client, the timing or you. Treat them as stories, not evidence. |
| Which steps need your judgment? | Yes, with an override log | Each beta produces dozens of judgment calls, so a few betas give plenty of observations |
| Which questions produce useful answers? | Partly | Enough to cut weak questions; the diagnostic adds the volume |
| What does typical misalignment look like? | No | That needs many companies, which is the diagnostic's job |

### The override log

Keep it from the first beta. Every time you change something the AI drafted, log one line: what the AI said, what you changed, why, and which step it was (interview follow-up, synthesis theme, divergence summary, schwerpunkt candidate, guardrail wording, flight log, pulse question).

- Review it after each beta. Steps you rarely override are candidates to hand to the AI. Steps you override every time are what clients pay you for.
- It applies your own guardrail rule to yourself: repeated overrides are information, not noise.
- Keep it in your private library, never in anything a client sees.

### The alignment diagnostic

A low-priced, self-serve product that runs without your time. Each leader takes the AI interview; an agent produces a divergence report showing where the team agrees, where it differs and which trade-offs are unsettled. It ends with one offer: help settling them, which is the Beta.

- **Where it sits:** its own page on franklinkinetics.com, linked from How It Works ("Not ready for the Beta? Start here") and from Start a conversation. It is the first step toward working with you. It is the upgraded version of the Rotor Diagnostic Pack on the product ladder. Not in the Labs store, whose buyers are households.
- **Who builds it:** the Labs agent crew, through the same approval gates as Lab products. Record decisions in the Labs-Agents repo.
- **What it collects:** the same wave 1 questions as the Interviewer Pack, so diagnostic data and beta data can be compared.
- **Data rule:** interviews run in the client's own AI. Decide before building how summaries are combined: by the client in their own AI (no hosting, but the CEO sees individual answers) or sent to you for the agent to combine and delete (more candour, but you hold data briefly). Either way, no report for fewer than three leaders, and quotes are unattributed.
- **What it must not do:** promise results, disclose the Rotor Core, or settle the strategy for them. It shows the gaps; the Beta closes them.
