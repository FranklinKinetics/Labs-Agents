### 2. The problem

Headline: **Strategy lives in documents. Decisions happen everywhere else.**

Most leadership teams put real work into strategy. Then it goes into a deck, and the deck goes into a folder. In a survey of more than 11,000 managers reported in Harvard Business Review, only about a third could list their company's top three priorities (Sull, Homkes and Sull, 2015).

Nobody orients their Tuesday around a mission statement. Strategy only counts where it shows up: in how people read the customer email, the hiring call, the thing that just broke. Setting that orientation is the CEO's core job.

Now add AI. Your people make dozens of decisions a day with ChatGPT, Claude or Copilot at their elbow. Those tools are fluent, fast, and know nothing about your strategy. Uploading the deck doesn't fix it. A vague document produces vague guidance, just faster.

### How results are measured

We report measures that can change within one quarter. We do not promise financial results. Those are written down as hypotheses at the start and tracked in later quarterly reviews.

**Decision consistency (a noise audit).** Before and after the live run, the Beta and comparison teams answer the same set of realistic decision cases, presented by an AI that also records the reasoning behind each answer. We compare how far apart their answers are. For reference: in an insurance company studied in *Noise* (Kahneman, Sibony and Sunstein, 2021), executives expected their underwriters' prices for the same case to differ by about 10%. The actual difference was about 55%.

**Supporting measures**

- Leadership agreement on the main effort
- Unprompted recall of the priorities
- Time spent compared with stated priorities
- Decision speed
- Early warning signals from the assumption tripwires
- Logged decisions that the guidance changed

### Why it works

#### Commander's intent

Militaries learned long ago that they can't control every decision. Once a plan meets reality, the people closest to the problem have to act on their own, often with no one senior to ask.

Their answer is commander's intent: a short, clear statement of what the mission is for and what matters most. With it, people can make the call in the moment. If the call isn't perfect, they can explain how they reasoned from the intent, and they aren't punished for using their judgment.

Business works the same way. The Rotor Sheet is your commander's intent, written down, and the AI tools help people reason back to it. If you want your people to act on their own, they need a strong sense of your intent. If you'd rather they wait for instructions, you don't need Rotor.

Rotor applies that principle to business.

#### The Rotor Framework

Most companies don't lack effort. They lack a shared direction for it. When every team works hard toward a slightly different goal, the effort cancels out: projects stall, priorities compete for the same people, and the leader ends up settling disputes the strategy should have settled.

The Rotor Framework fixes that by giving everyone the same reference point: one main effort, clear rules for the common trade-offs, and the assumptions the plan depends on. With those in place, people can move quickly and still pull in the same direction, and leadership sees early when an assumption stops holding.

We picture it as a spinning top. While it turns around one axis, it stays upright. Lose the axis, or let friction build, and it starts to wobble.

That simplicity is deliberate. A few clear ideas and one memorable image make both the strategy and the way your organization actually moves easier for each person to remember. A forty-page plan is forgotten by Friday. A shared picture is still there when someone has to make a call on their own.

#### How the AI behaves

The same reference point goes into your AI tools. Every client's AI works from two things: the Rotor Core, a licensed set of reasoning models that is the same for every client, and your own Rotor Sheet. It is set up to:

- ask questions rather than give instructions
- assume the person knows more about their situation than it does
- raise issues in proportion to the stakes
- accept a decision once the person has made it
- never steer covertly

#### Research behind it

None of this is new on its own. Rotor brings together well-tested work on strategy and decision-making:

- Michael Porter: strategy means choosing what not to do.
- Richard Rumelt: good strategy contains a guiding policy.
- Kathleen Eisenhardt and Donald Sull: simple rules work better than detailed rulebooks in fast-moving markets.
- Rita McGrath: treat plans as assumptions and test them.
- Stephen Bungay: mission command applied to business.
- Daniel Kahneman, Olivier Sibony and Cass Sunstein: professional judgments vary far more than organizations expect.

## FAQ

[NOTE: Accordion on the home page, section 9. Can repeat on How It Works.]

**We already run EOS (or OKRs). Why add this?**

Keep it. Your operating system already does the hard thinking: priorities, targets, accountability. Rotor takes what you have decided and makes it usable at the moment of decision, for your people and for the AI tools they now work with. You have a strategy page. Your AI doesn't.

**Can't we just upload our strategy deck to ChatGPT?**

You can, and you'll get guidance as clear as the deck. Most strategy documents are written to persuade a board, not to settle a Tuesday decision. Rotor turns them into a main effort, explicit trade-off rules and beliefs with tripwires, which is something an AI tool can actually apply.

**Is our strategy safe?**

Your strategy stays in tools your company already controls, under your existing agreements with those vendors. In the Beta, Franklin Kinetics never hosts your strategy data, and the basic setup needs no new software. The alignment diagnostic briefly holds each leader's summary, with names removed, and deletes it once your report is delivered.

**Will staff feel monitored?**

Rotor guides decisions. It doesn't watch people. Every instruction the AI follows is written in plain language your staff can read, and nothing steers them covertly. Rotor shapes how the company decides. It doesn't score how individuals perform.

**Will the AI start bossing people around?**

No. It is set up like a well-briefed first officer, not a compliance officer. It assumes the person knows more than it does, raises considerations as questions, escalates only as far as the stakes require, and accepts an informed decision once it's made.

**How much of our leaders' time does this take?**

Very little calendar time. There are three meetings: a kickoff, the Mirror and the review. Leaders do their interviews when it suits them, in two or three short sessions by voice or text, and the CEO gets a short weekly note instead of a status meeting.

**An AI interviews our leaders?**

Yes. It asks the questions and the follow-ups, so each leader can answer candidly, at their own pace, before hearing anyone else's view. I design the questions, read every transcript and make every judgment. It runs on the AI platform you already use, and it gives you a first look at what the Beta delivers: an AI that works from your strategy.
