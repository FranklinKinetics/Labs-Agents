## 1. The fictional company

**Larchmere Fabrication Ltd.** (invented). Custom steel fabrication on the Prairies, about 85 people, owner-led, second generation. Most work is short-run parts for local equipment makers and contractors. A large agricultural equipment maker has asked them to quote a multi-year supply contract.

Five leaders took the interview. The disagreement built into their answers:
- **The big contract.** Two want it badly. One thinks it would swamp the shop. Two are uneasy for other reasons.
- **Rush jobs.** Everyone complains about them. No one agrees on who can say no.
- **Where work waits.** Each leader names a different bottleneck. Several point at the same one without knowing it: decisions waiting on the owner.

# Leadership alignment report · Larchmere Fabrication
*Fictional sample*
2026-11-10 · Based on interviews with 5 leaders

## How to read this report
Each of you spoke separately with an AI interviewer. No person read those conversations. Your summaries were combined here, then deleted. Quotes are unattributed, and nothing in this report says who said what.

This report shows where your views line up and where they don't. It does not tell you which view is right. That's for your team to settle.

## 1. Where the team agrees
- **Rush jobs hurt.** Most of you named rush work as a problem, whether for the schedule or for margin. "Every job is a VIP, so no job is."
- **Quotes are too slow.** Most of you said work or sales wait on quotes and drawings. You agree on the symptom. You don't agree on the cause (see section 6).
- **You want steadier work.** Most of you described the business as living job to job, or busier without being better off. "We're running on Tim's and overtime."
- **Some decisions don't hold.** All of you could name a rule from the past year that faded within weeks.

## 2. Where the team differs

### What you each said the main effort is
- "Protect margin and cash this year, because we're busy but we're not making more money."
- "Win the Prairie Ag supply contract, because it's the steady base load that gets us off the rush-job treadmill."
- "Standardize our most common parts and quotes, so we stop reinventing the same bracket every week."
- "Get on-time delivery back above where it was two years ago, because our regulars are the business and we're losing their trust."
- "Land Prairie Ag and two more equipment makers like them, so we stop living job to job."

Five leaders gave four different answers. Some of you point at growth through a large new customer. Others point at margin, delivery or standard work. Most of these share a goal underneath: steadier, more predictable work. You differ on how to get there, and in what order.

### Other places your views split
**The Prairie Ag contract.** Some of you see it as the way off the treadmill. Others worry it would swamp the shop, or that it only works at the right price. No one is opposed outright. The disagreement is about conditions.

**Who you are.** Some of you describe a custom shop that does anything well. Others want a production shop with standard parts. "We sell like Costco and build like a jeweller."

**What existing customers will tolerate.** Some of you believe regulars will accept longer lead times. Others believe they are already losing patience.

## 3. Trade-offs that aren't settled
- **Big new client versus existing customers.** Asked which way to lean when the two collide, some of you said the big client, because a chance like this doesn't come twice. Others said existing customers, because the shop is already behind. One answer was "it depends on the margin."
- **Volume versus margin.** Some of you are willing to price sharper on volume work. Others say volume at a thin price is not a win, and that rush work already costs more than you charge.
- **Custom versus standard.** Giving up some fully custom work would free engineering time and shorten quotes. Not everyone has said they're ready to give it up.
- **Growth now versus capacity first.** Some of you would grow first and add capacity as needed. Others would fix delivery first, then grow.

## 4. What these differences may lead to
These follow from what you told us. They are possibilities to test, not predictions.

- **Rush work keeps jumping the queue.** You haven't settled who can approve a rush job, or which customer comes first when they collide. While that stays open, rush orders may keep slipping everything behind them, as the past rules on rush work did. "The shop is a funnel with a cork in it."
- **The contract quote and the shop plan may rest on different beliefs.** Some of you would price sharper for volume. Others believe the shop can't take the work without a second shift, or that rush work already costs more than you charge. A quote built on one view, and a shop plan built on another, may show up in the warning signs you named: overtime climbing and margin slipping.
- **People get different answers depending on who they ask.** With "which customer comes first" unsettled, the floor, sales and estimating may each lean their own way. "We're a hockey team that changes lines whenever someone yells from the bench."
- **Standard parts may stall again.** Standardizing needs sales to offer standard options. While "custom shop or production shop" is open, a drawing library may go unfilled again, as it did before.

## 4. Vera's check, and what the sample taught us

**Checked:** no names or roles in the report. No exact counts beyond the leader total. No advice. No main effort recommended. No money claims. No "pilot", "schwerpunkt" or "ROI". Labelled fictional.

**Three things the sample exposed. They feed back into deliverable 4:**

1. **"Decisions wait for the owner" can point at people.** Any leader who isn't the owner could have said it. But the owner reading it knows the team is talking about them, and that's the point. It stays. The rule held: it never says who said it.
2. **Some decisions that didn't stick identify a department.** "Rush surcharge, waived by sales" would have named a team. The report dropped "by sales". Proposed addition to the agent's rules: *Remove department or team names from decisions that didn't stick.*
3. **"Five leaders gave four different answers."** That's a count about main efforts, not about people. It's allowed and it's the most striking line in the report. Proposed addition: *You may say how many different main efforts there are.*

**Accuracy check (2026-11-10):** every claim in the report was traced back to the five blocks. Three overstatements were corrected before this draft: two "all of you" claims became "most of you", and "all five aim at the same thing" became "most of these share a goal". Proposed addition to the agent's check sheet: *Every "all" claim is supported by every block.*

## 5. Added after gate 5: what the differences may lead to

Brandon (2026-11-10): he'd want the report to go one step further, into second-order effects and risks that come from leaders pursuing different main efforts. But not so far that it sounds invented or unreal and causes cognitive dissonance.

**How to keep it grounded: one step, from evidence.** Each effect must join two things leaders actually said:
- a **split** (from "Where the team differs" or "Trade-offs that aren't settled"), and
- a **friction point, warning sign or decision that didn't stick** that leaders already named.

If the agent can't name both, it doesn't write the effect. No chains ("which leads to… which leads to…"). No money estimates: at most "a possibility your team could test". No advice on what to do about it.

**Proposed addition to the synthesis instructions (deliverable 4):**

```markdown
# What these differences may lead to
- Write 2 to 4 effects. Each one joins a split from sections 2 or 3 with a friction point, warning sign or decision that didn't stick that leaders named.
- Write each as: "Because <the split>, <what leaders already see> may continue or grow."
- One step only. Never chain effects. Never invent a scenario leaders didn't describe.
- Use "may", never "will". Never estimate money, time or results.
- Never say what to do about it.
- If you can't join a split to something leaders named, leave the effect out.
```
